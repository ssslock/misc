Abstract

Uncertainty calibration, the alignment of predictive confidence with accuracy, is essential for the reliable deployment of machine learning systems in real-world applications. However, current models often fail to achieve this goal, generating responses that are overconfident, inaccurate or even fabricated. Here we show that the widely adopted initialization method in deep learning—long regarded as standard practice—is, in fact, a primary source of overconfidence. To address this problem, we introduce a neurodevelopment-inspired warm-up strategy that inherently resolves uncertainty-related issues without requiring pre- or post-processing. In our approach, networks are first briefly trained on random noise and random labels before being exposed to real data. This warm-up phase yields optimal calibration, ensuring that confidence remains well aligned with accuracy throughout subsequent training. Moreover, the resulting networks demonstrate high proficiency in the identification of ‘unknown’ inputs, providing a robust solution for uncertainty calibration in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution contexts.

Main

Recent advances in deep neural networks have substantially improved their ability to learn with high accuracy1. For example, deep learning models achieved human-level accuracy in classifying natural images over a decade ago2,3,4,5. These models are now being applied to a wide range of real-world problems that involve complex decision-making, such as autonomous driving6, medical diagnosis7 and financial engineering8. In these applications, simply inferring the correct answer is insufficient; an additional dimension of information, confidence or uncertainty, is crucial to predict answers with calibrated probabilities that accurately reflect the likelihood of correctness (Fig. 1a). However, modern deep neural networks are often poorly calibrated for confidence estimation9,10,11. Ironically, the increase in model capacity enables accurate performance but often negatively impacts confidence calibration9. Specifically, many recent models frequently exhibit overconfidence, even with out-of-distribution (OOD) samples, despite lacking knowledge about them12,13. This miscalibration leads to poor decision-making in real-world applications14,15. For example, hallucinations in large language models (LLMs)16,17, where the model confidently produces false and unsubstantiated outputs18,19, are a well-known issue closely linked to the miscalibration of neural networks20,21.

Previous studies have tried to address calibration issues through additional pre- or post-processing techniques. However, these methods require additional, computationally expensive steps to obtain calibrated confidence9,22,23,24,25. Many state-of-the-art algorithms use auxiliary networks or score functions to differentiate between overconfident predictions for unseen OOD data and those for in-distribution (ID) data26,27,28. In general, the fundamental problem is that previous approaches typically treat ID and OOD data separately, which essentially recalibrates the confidence values through separate calculation processes to achieve the desired results.

We can ask a more fundamental question: what causes uncertainty miscalibration in deep neural networks? Unlike machines, human intelligence naturally exhibits meta-cognition, enabling the estimation of uncertainty and awareness of what is ‘known’ and ‘unknown’29,30,31,32,33. Hence, what is the underlying mechanism of uncertainty calibration in the brain, and how can artificial neural networks learn brain-like uncertainty calibration, possibly without post-processing or auxiliary computation? To address this, we build on our previous work34, which introduced an initial training phase using random noise, inspired by the biological prenatal stage. Specifically, we hypothesize that these initial pre-calibrations could facilitate the learning of calibrated uncertainty.

In this study, we demonstrate that the commonly used method of random initialization in deep learning—long regarded as a standard technique—is, in fact, the underlying cause of network uncertainty miscalibration. The solution to this miscalibration issue can be achieved simply through an initialization strategy that deviates from conventional practices—warm-up training with random noise and labels, without additional pre- or post-processing methods. Our findings show that randomly initialized neural networks often exhibit high confidence even when they lack sufficient knowledge, but training with random noise effectively calibrates this confidence to uniform chance levels across the input spaces. As a result, the networks learn calibrated probabilities, with accuracy and confidence aligned throughout subsequent data training. Additionally, pre-calibrated networks show lower confidence when encountering unknown data, which helps in detecting OOD samples. Overall, our results suggest that warm-up training with random noise provides a simple yet powerful strategy for addressing uncertainty calibration issues.
Results
Failure of confidence calibration in deep neural networks

To investigate the pattern of confidence miscalibration in deep neural network models, we first used a feed-forward neural network designed for pattern classification of three-channel natural images (32 × 32 × 3) into ten categories (Fig. 1b and Methods). The network consists of multiple hidden layers with rectified linear unit nonlinearities and a final classification layer that uses the SoftMax function. For a given image sample, the model outputs probabilities for each object category, and the predicted label corresponds to the category with the maximum probability. The model also provides a measure of prediction confidence, which is derived from the probability assigned to the predicted category. The predictive uncertainty can be quantified as the difference between the confidence value and one. Ideally, a neural network is expected to output calibrated confidence, where the confidence level accurately reflects the actual likelihood of the prediction being correct (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1). By contrast, a poorly calibrated network may output confidence levels that do not correspond to the actual likelihood of correctness, leading to potential misinterpretations of the model’s predictions.

We trained the feed-forward neural network using a subset of the Canadian Institute For Advanced Research (CIFAR)-10 dataset35, which consists of natural images labelled with ten classes representing animals and objects. After training, we evaluated the model’s predictions and confidence using test images that were not part of the training set. Specifically, we investigated whether the confidence level of the trained network accurately reflects the likelihood of correctness (Supplementary Fig. 1). By measuring binned confidence values and the corresponding expected accuracy within each binned trial prediction, we constructed a reliability diagram25,36 to visualize the model’s calibration (Fig. 1c). In this diagram, ideal calibration appears as an identity function for which the estimated confidence corresponds to the actual probability of correctness.

We investigated the pattern of miscalibration by training neural networks of varying complexity with different training data sizes (Fig. 1d). To quantitatively measure the degree of miscalibration, we calculated the expected calibration error (ECE)37, which averages the difference between accuracy and confidence in each bin, weighted by the number of samples. In general, we observed a substantial gap between ideal calibration and the model’s outputs—the model’s accuracy was lower than its confidence level, demonstrating the model’s tendency for overconfidence in most conditions. We found that both model complexity and training data size systematically affect confidence calibration. Specifically, the degree of miscalibration increases as the training data size decreases and model complexity increases, suggesting that miscalibration occurs when the training data are insufficient in relation to the network’s complexity. This result explains why state-of-the-art models with high complexity struggle with mismatch between confidence and accuracy. Recent network architectures often increase in depth to enhance learning capacity, but the available data size does not scale accordingly. In real-world applications, acquiring large datasets is costly and computationally demanding, making miscalibration inevitable in deep learning models.
Warm-up training with random noise for confidence calibration

The biological brain begins its learning process even before birth, before encountering sensory inputs, through spontaneous neural activity38 (Fig. 2a). During this early stage, the brain optimizes its neural connections by transmitting this spontaneous activity to higher cortical regions, and this prenatal learning is observed across multiple brain areas involved in sensory systems38,39,40,41 (Fig. 2b). In particular, the spontaneous activities take on various spatiotemporal forms, such as waves39 or local synchronization40. Importantly, these random activities are independent of external stimuli and bear no association with the real world or specific objectives, yet they critically mediate the initial formation of neuronal circuits42,43. Thus, this process can be seen as a form of warm-up training with intrinsic random noise34. Here we demonstrate that a warm-up training algorithm inspired by this biological process facilitates the preconditioning of networks, calibrating their initial confidence to a chance level.

We trained neural networks using noise inputs randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution and random labels sampled from a uniform distribution (Fig. 2c). During training with random noise, we observed that the loss gradually decreased, whereas accuracy remained at the chance level (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2). When the network was subsequently trained with real data after the warm-up training, we observed a more substantial reduction in test loss, which was significantly lower compared with networks trained without random noise warm-up (Fig. 2d, without versus with random noise warm-up, nnet = 30 independently initialized networks, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired networks initialized with the same seed, without assuming normality, W = 0, P = 1.73 × 10−6, r = 0.873). These results demonstrate that random noise training facilitates faster and more effective loss reduction during subsequent training with real data.

Next, we investigated whether random noise warm-up could improve the calibration of probabilities, aligning confidence with accuracy. Using each network’s predictions and the corresponding confidences, we constructed reliability diagrams (Fig. 2e), which showed that the network trained with random noise had outputs that were closer to ideal calibration than networks trained on data alone. Additionally, we confirmed that the networks exhibited significantly lower calibration error compared with those trained only with data (Fig. 2e (inset), without versus with random noise warm-up, nnet = 30, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 1.73 × 10−6, r = 0.873). We validated that our random noise warm-up consistently improves model calibration across a wide range of tasks, including image classification (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1), image segmentation (Supplementary Fig. 4) and language generation (Supplementary Fig. 5). Beyond networks trained with standard backpropagation44, we also evaluated networks trained with a biologically plausible feedback alignment algorithm45 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Miscalibration was particularly pronounced under this approximated error backpropagation, yet random noise warm-up substantially improved confidence calibration. These results confirm that miscalibration is a widespread issue across datasets, task domains and training algorithms, and that random noise warm-up provides a general solution for enhancing confidence calibration.

We then extended this analysis to networks with varying depths and sizes of training data. We found that random noise warm-up had a significant effect, regardless of the network depth or training data size (Fig. 2f, without versus with random noise warm-up, nnet = 10, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 5.06 × 10−3, r = 0.886; the statistical test was conducted for every combination of network depth and training data size). Random noise training successfully reduced calibration error across all conditions examined. In particular, the effect was particularly pronounced for networks with deeper structures trained on smaller datasets, where confidence miscalibration is more common. We further extended the analysis to ResNet4 and DenseNet5 families, as well as vision transformers46, confirming that calibration error increases with model size. Random noise warm-up effectively mitigated this effect (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 2 (baseline)). We also found that it prevents miscalibration when training data are limited, even in complex networks such as ResNet-18 (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 3 (baseline)). In particular, the calibration effect of random noise warm-up is comparable with, or even surpasses, previous post-calibration techniques9,23, despite requiring no hold-out dataset (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, because our approach functions not merely as a calibration method but as a general initialization strategy, it can be combined with existing state-of-the-art algorithms to achieve superior calibration. We confirmed that the effects of random noise warm-up are not sensitive to the specific choice of noise distribution and are consistently reproduced with various types of random or structured noise (Supplementary Fig. 10) or different input variances (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Additionally, we validated that these additional warm-up steps are substantially shorter than conventional large-scale data pretraining or downstream task training, imposing negligible computational overhead (Supplementary Fig. 12). We applied a short random noise warm-up (0.25M examples are seen) when training ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 in both the scratch-training scenario (2.5M examples are seen) and the fine-tuning scenario (1M examples are seen) (Fig. 2g,h). In the scratch-training scenario, incorporating random noise warm-up enabled the model to achieve superior calibration (Fig. 2g, task only versus random + task, nnet = 20, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 8.86 × 10−5, r = 0.877), and a similar effect was observed in the fine-tuning scenario following large-scale ImageNet pretraining (120M examples are seen) (Fig. 2g, pretraining + task versus pretraining + random + task, nnet = 20, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 8.86 × 10−5, r = 0.877). In addition, random noise warm-up enabled networks to achieve comparable or higher test performance (Fig. 2h, task only versus random + task, nnet = 20, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 64, P = 0.126, r = 0.342; pretraining + task versus pretraining + random + task, nnet = 20, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 8.85 × 10−5, r = 0.877). These results suggest that random noise warm-up improves calibration without sacrificing test performance, adding only negligible computational cost. Moreover, the effect of random noise warm-up cannot be replicated by conventional large-scale data pretraining.
Initialization with random noise warm-up for uncertainty calibration

To better understand how warm-up training with random noise affects confidence calibration, we further explored the characteristics of randomly initialized, untrained networks and those that were randomly trained, before exposure to real data. For this purpose, we introduced a simplified toy model network with a two-dimensional (2D) input space (Fig. 3a), allowing for the visualization of the input feature space (Fig. 3b). Under these conditions, we observed that the initial state of a conventionally processed network—randomly initialized47 and untrained—displayed a highly biased distribution of confidence across the input space (Fig. 3b, left). Moreover, we confirmed that this initial miscalibration was consistent across multiple trials with different random initializations. By contrast, networks trained with random noise displayed a more homogeneous confidence distribution across the input space, effectively calibrating to the chance level (Fig. 3b, right). This result demonstrates that training with random noise helps mitigate overconfidence by calibrating confidence levels across the entire input space, bringing them closer to chance (Fig. 3c, untrained versus random noise trained, nsample = 1,000 per group, two-sided Mann–Whitney U test for two independent groups without assuming normality, U = 975,129.5, P = 1.67 × 10−296, r = 0.823). We also observed that untrained networks could exhibit initial prediction biases towards specific output classes due to fluctuations in probability. However, random noise training significantly reduced this initial class bias, leading to a more uniform probability distribution across the classes (Fig. 3d, untrained versus random noise trained, nnet = 30, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 62, P = 4.53 × 10−4, r = 0.640).

We next examined this initial overconfidence of randomly initialized networks by analysing the relationship between network activation and SoftMax output probabilities (Fig. 3e–g; Supplementary Note 1 shows the corresponding theoretical analysis). In randomly initialized networks, logits were widely distributed (Fig. 3e). Their large magnitudes, arising from high variance, caused saturation due to the nonlinear SoftMax function, concentrating the output probabilities near 0 and 1 (Fig. 3f). This saturation indicates that untrained networks exhibit overconfidence, with probabilities biased towards 1 (Fig. 3g). Random noise warm-up effectively mitigated this initial overconfidence (Fig. 3h–j; Supplementary Note 2 shows the corresponding theoretical analysis). Random noise training reshaped the logit distribution by concentrating values in the linear region of the SoftMax function (Fig. 3h) and suppressing large-magnitude logits in the long tail (Fig. 3i), resulting in output probabilities peaking near the chance level (Fig. 3j). Importantly, random noise training did not appreciably alter the weight distribution but substantially changed the activation patterns across the network hierarchy (Supplementary Fig. 13). Unlike conventional initialization, random noise warm-up gradually reduced activation scales across the hierarchy, compressing the final-layer logits to produce outputs close to chance level.

Next, we extended this analysis to models with higher-dimensional input spaces (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Specifically, we used a model with input features of 32 × 32 × 3 dimensions and output SoftMax probabilities for ten classes, allowing it to train on the CIFAR-10 data. We first measured the probability outputs for random noise inputs in both untrained and randomly trained networks (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Consistent with the toy model results, we observed overconfidence and class bias in untrained networks (Supplementary Fig. 14b, left), whereas such biases were barely detectable in randomly trained networks (Supplementary Fig. 14b, right). We confirmed that these differences aligned with the toy model results, both in terms of confidence distribution (Supplementary Fig. 14c) and class bias (Supplementary Fig. 14d). We further extended our analysis to various network architectures with differing numbers of outputs (Supplementary Fig. 15a) and depths (Supplementary Fig. 15b). These results confirmed that the initial overconfidence is a universal feature of randomly initialized networks, which random noise training effectively mitigates by reducing confidence to the chance level. In particular, the SoftMax outputs of modern architectures, including ResNet, DenseNet and vision transformers, were highly polarized in their untrained state, with most predictions being overconfident. Random noise training consistently eliminated this bias (Supplementary Fig. 16).

We further investigated whether the pre-calibration effect observed with random noise training extends to unseen, more realistic data. Specifically, we examined how the network’s confidence on the CIFAR-10 and Street View House Numbers (SVHN)48 datasets changes during random noise training (Supplementary Fig. 17a, left) by measuring the network’s predictions for real data after each epoch of training. In particular, we observed that the confidence for unseen CIFAR-10 data was properly calibrated throughout the random noise training phase, approaching the chance level, whereas accuracy remained at the chance level (Supplementary Fig. 17a, right). We confirmed that this trend was consistent across different unseen datasets, including CIFAR-10 and SVHN (Supplementary Fig. 17b). The network maximized uncertainty for unseen data, with accuracy remaining at the chance level, thereby aligning confidence with accuracy. As a result, the calibration error—the discrepancy between confidence and accuracy—was significantly reduced for unseen data (Supplementary Fig. 17c). These findings demonstrate that conventionally initialized networks exhibit overconfidence and class bias even before training, whereas random noise training effectively eliminates this initial bias and calibrates confidence to the chance level, maximizing uncertainty before actual learning. This suggests that random noise training can serve as an initialization strategy, offering an alternative to conventional random initialization47.

Finally, we confirmed that this initial calibration stabilizes gradients (Supplementary Fig. 18) and smooths the loss landscape at initialization (Supplementary Fig. 19). Consequently, uncertainty-calibrated initialization via random noise warm-up enables stable learning within a smoother loss landscape (Supplementary Fig. 20) and ultimately converges at flatter minima (Supplementary Fig. 21). We also visualized the relationship between loss landscape and calibration (Supplementary Fig. 22) and observed that random noise training rapidly shifts the network into a well-calibrated region. As a result, subsequent task learning occurs in a parameter space that is already well calibrated. This analysis demonstrates the role of random noise warm-up in shaping the calibrated learning landscape and highlights how its learning dynamics differ from those of conventionally initialized networks. Importantly, we confirmed that these benefits of random noise warm-up are not merely due to changes in the effective learning rate or weight distribution (Supplementary Fig. 23).
Aligning confidence levels with actual accuracy

To better understand the impact of pre-calibration, we examined the learning dynamics of networks during data training, by analysing the training trajectory in a 2D plane of accuracy and loss (Fig. 4a). The trajectory showed clear differences between networks with and without random noise training, primarily due to the initial loss reduction during warm-up. Specifically, for points on both curves with the same accuracy, the randomly trained network exhibited lower loss than the network trained solely on data. By selecting arbitrary points of identical accuracy (Fig. 4a, (i) and (ii)), we analysed the reliability diagram and calibration error for both networks. We found that networks with random noise warm-up had better confidence calibration compared with those without warm-up, even with the same accuracy (Fig. 4b, with versus without random noise warm-up, nnet = 30, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 1.73 × 10−6, r = 0.873). Similarly, when selecting arbitrary points with the same total training epochs (Fig. 4a, (iii) and (iv)), networks warmed up again demonstrated better confidence calibration (Fig. 4c, without versus with random noise warm-up, nnet = 30, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 0, P = 1.73 × 10−6, r = 0.873). These results highlight how random noise warm-up influences the dynamics of subsequent learning, particularly by reducing initial loss.

We next explored the relationship between confidence and accuracy throughout the training process (Fig. 4d). In random-noise-trained networks, confidence and accuracy remained well aligned, consistently following the diagonal line during training (Fig. 4d, with). By contrast, networks without warm-up did not exhibit this alignment (Fig. 4d (without) and Fig. 4e). In particular, a substantial disparity between confidence and accuracy was initially observed due to early miscalibration, but this gap quickly diminished and disappeared during random noise warm-up (Fig. 4d, random). This alignment of confidence and accuracy supports more synchronized learning during subsequent data training (Fig. 4f). Thus, calibrating confidence to the chance level through random noise warm-up ensures proper confidence calibration.
Detection of OOD samples using calibrated network confidence

Finally, we examined the effect of random noise warm-up on detecting OOD data. OOD samples refer to ‘unseen’ data the model has not been trained on, whereas ID samples are those the model has been trained to recognize (Fig. 5a). After training on CIFAR-10 (ID), we evaluated the model’s response to unseen SVHN data (OOD). Networks without random noise warm-up exhibited high confidence on OOD samples, even though they had no prior knowledge of them (Fig. 5b, left). By contrast, networks with random noise warm-up showed significantly lower confidence on OOD samples, aligning closer to the chance level—ideal for unknown data. We confirmed that randomly trained networks had lower average confidence for OOD samples, closer to chance level (Fig. 5b, right; without versus with random noise warm-up, nsample = 10,000 per group, two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, U = 98,908,786, P = 4.94 × 10−324, r = 0.847).

We further explored how calibrated confidence for both ID and OOD samples can improve OOD detection. To achieve this, we implemented a simple framework in which raw confidence values were used to distinguish ID from OOD samples (Fig. 5c), with samples below an arbitrary threshold being classified as OOD. We then assessed the OOD detection performance across various thresholds and used these results to plot the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 5d, left). To quantify performance, we calculated the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) (Fig. 5d, right) and found that random noise warm-up significantly improved OOD detection (Fig. 5d, right; without versus with random noise warm-up, nnet = 30, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 60, P = 3.88 × 10−4, r = 0.648).

We extended our analysis to ResNet and DenseNet families, as well as vision transformers, and confirmed that random noise warm-up generally enhances OOD detection (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 4 (baseline)). In addition, we confirmed that random noise warm-up is effective in improving OOD detection performance in complex networks, such as ResNet-18, regardless of the training data size (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 5 (baseline)). In particular, beyond confidence-based OOD detection, networks trained with random noise substantially outperformed conventionally initialized networks in advanced OOD detection frameworks, including temperature scaling, ODIN13 and energy score28 methods (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Moreover, random noise warm-up reduced predictive uncertainty and achieved superior OOD detection within the conformal prediction framework49,50 (Supplementary Fig. 27). Taken together, these results suggest that random noise warm-up and the resulting initial calibration provide a crucial foundation for meta-cognitive ability in neural networks, enabling robust discrimination between known and unknown data without additional processing or augmented learning.
Discussion

The ability to estimate uncertainty and recognize the unknown is crucial for real-world intelligent systems. Human intelligence naturally exhibits meta-cognition—the ability to assess probabilistic confidence32,33 and distinguish between what is known and unknown. This capacity plays a crucial role in complex human behaviours, particularly in decision-making29,30,31. Without this meta-cognitive ability to estimate uncertainty, intelligent systems may make critical errors in real-world scenarios in which incorrect decisions can be costly14,15. Despite recent advances in performance2,3,4,5, deep neural networks continue to struggle with uncertainty calibration9,10,11, as our findings have demonstrated. This issue poses a critical challenge for integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into daily life, despite its advanced capabilities.

Hallucinations in LLMs16,17 are a prominent example of how miscalibrated uncertainty can make AI systems unreliable. Although LLMs exhibit expert-level proficiency across various domains, such as science, programming and healthcare, they often produce incorrect answers with high confidence, making it difficult to trust their outputs in critical situations18,19. Beyond LLMs, miscalibration has long been a challenge in applying AI across real-world domains. For instance, although autonomous vehicle prototypes emerged over a decade ago, their integration into daily life remains difficult due to reliability concerns, highlighted by a fatal accident caused by the control system misinterpreting an object51. Similar issues arise in healthcare in which models that classify diseases from radiological images often make overconfident but incorrect predictions when presented with outlier inputs52. Relying on such overconfident judgements in medical procedures could jeopardize patient safety. These examples highlight how miscalibrated predictions can lead to catastrophic decision-making errors, emphasizing the need to address miscalibration issues before AI is deployed in critical applications.

Our results demonstrate that these miscalibrations of uncertainty are inevitable in modern deep neural networks as they become more complex to enhance their learning capacity2,3,4,5. We found that both dataset size and model complexity systematically influence calibration quality—when the dataset is insufficient, large and complex models experience serious calibration errors. This raises the question of whether an extremely large dataset is always necessary for achieving optimal calibration as model complexity increases. However, acquiring high-quality training data is often costly in the real world, and in some domains, the available data are inherently limited, such as text data in minority languages or medical data on rare diseases. Given these practical challenges, relying solely on expanding dataset size is not a feasible solution as model complexity grows. Our proposed method addresses this by decoupling miscalibration issues from both dataset size and model complexity, offering a simple yet effective strategy for modern deep neural networks as they evolve with increasing parameters.

A long-standing question in machine learning is how to properly initialize neural networks for efficient learning, as the network may converge to a local minimum or diverge depending on the initial condition53,54. Previous studies have shown that adjusting initialization can help networks converge with higher accuracy47,55, leading to the development of optimized techniques for initialization, a common starting point for training. However, our findings suggest that these traditional methods may not be ideal for learning due to issues with confidence calibration—networks initialized by conventional methods tend to suffer from overconfidence and biases towards specific outputs. The widely adopted practice of random initialization in deep learning is actually a key factor contributing to the miscalibration of network uncertainty; however, training with random noise offers a straightforward and effective solution, thereby proposing an alternative approach for network initialization. This approach is applicable to all types of network model, and remains effective regardless of the model size, complexity or other characteristics. Unlike previous uncertainty calibration methods that rely on auxiliary computation or post-processing9,22,23,24,25,26,27,28, our findings demonstrate that simply altering the initialization of learning through random noise training substantially changes the learning dynamics, aligning confidence with accuracy.

Our work on warm-up training with random noise is inspired by the prenatal developmental process of biological brains. Although spontaneous neural activity in the brain38,39,40,41 is recognized as important for development, its functional role remains unclear. Recently, artificial neural networks have been considered as a powerful framework for understanding biological neural processing56,57,58. Our results not only provide an effective and practical solution to calibration issues in machine learning models but also offer insights into biological developmental processes34,39,59,60,61,62,63. In addition to using the backpropagation algorithm44, we explored the benefits of random noise training within biologically plausible learning rules, such as feedback alignment45. Interestingly, we observed severe miscalibration when using local learning rules, but random noise warm-up improved calibration substantially, consistent with our primary findings. Our recent research also indicated that initial calibration and conditioning through random noise warm-up can significantly enhance the learning efficiency of biologically plausible learning systems34. These findings suggest that the pre-sensory learning process in the biological brain may involve learning the unknown before acquiring knowledge through sensory experience, potentially marking a key developmental step towards meta-cognition.

Although the current study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our model has not yet been validated through real-world deployments or large-scale benchmarking, and the experiments were conducted on relatively simple tasks and smaller-scale networks compared with state-of-the-art systems, such as LLMs. Because training a single LLM can incur computational costs on the order of millions of dollars, it is generally impractical to systematically investigate their calibration properties within a typical academic laboratory setting. Nevertheless, we believe that the findings presented here will generalize to more complex, real-world models across domains, including computer vision, natural language processing and beyond. A key characteristic shared across various architectures and task domains is that their outputs ultimately take the form of probability distributions. Our warm-up training method is, therefore, broadly applicable as long as the model produces probabilistic outputs for decision-making, irrespective of its specific structure, scale or application domain. Looking ahead, applying our approach to a range of commercial AI systems to further validate and refine these findings represents an important direction for future research.

In summary, we conclude that warm-up training with random noise effectively pre-calibrates network uncertainty, enabling subsequent learning with aligned accuracy and confidence. These results highlight the importance of the initial state of neural networks for effective data learning47,53, as well as the significance of the early developmental process before encountering data34,64. Our findings provide insights into uncertainty calibration, offering potential solutions to challenges arising from calibration issues.
Methods
Neural network model

We used a multilayer feed-forward model as a simplified representative of conventional neural networks to investigate the miscalibration problem and the effect of random noise warm-up. The model consists of multiple nonlinear layers, each performing a series of operations: multiplying weights, adding biases and passing through a rectified linear unit activation function. To mitigate overfitting, batch normalization was applied to every layer. The hidden layer size was fixed at 256 neurons, with the layer depth varied across different experiments. The network weights were randomly initialized following a Gaussian distribution, with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation determined to control the gain across layers, as per the standard initialization method47. The biases were initialized to zero. We observed that our results were fairly consistent regardless of hyperparameter settings, such as the hidden layer size, under typical conditions.
Warm-up training with random noise

The random noise input was sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. We set the noise size to 32 × 32 × 3, matching the size of the input data and the input dimension of the neural networks. The corresponding labels for the random noise were also randomly sampled from a uniform distribution and one-hot encoded. Both random noise inputs and their labels were resampled at each iteration and were not paired with one another. During training, the random noise inputs were fed into the neural network, and the model output was obtained. The error was calculated as the distance between the model output and random labels, and the network was trained to minimize this error, similar to conventional training procedures.
Subsequent training with real data

After training the neural network with random noise, we proceeded to train it with real data. We used a subset of the CIFAR-10 (ref. 35) dataset, which consists of 32 × 32 × 3 natural images representing ten categories: airplanes, automobiles, birds, cats, deer, dogs, frogs, horses, ships and trucks. In the primary analysis, we used a training subset of 4,000 samples to assess the robustness of calibration following random noise warm-up training under challenging conditions. To investigate the effects of training data size and model capacity, we used feed-forward networks with depths of 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 and trained them on CIFAR-10 subsets of sizes 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000, 8,000, 16,000 and 32,000.

For both random noise training and subsequent training with real data, we followed commonly used neural network training procedures. We applied standard backpropagation with the Adam optimizer, using learning rates between 0.0001 and 0.00001 and betas of (0.9, 0.999). To prevent overfitting, we used weight decay with a decay constant of 0.001. During the analysis, we compared the networks trained with random noise to those trained solely on data, without any random noise warm-up. All conditions, except for the random noise warm-up, were carefully controlled to isolate the effect of the random noise warm-up.

Additionally, we analysed the neural network training using a feedback alignment45 algorithm, as a biologically plausible alternative to backpropagation that does not require weight transport. In feedback alignment, random synaptic feedback is used to compute the error signal, instead of using forward weights. Results obtained using this method are presented in Supplementary Information.

Noise warm-up in ResNet scratch training and fine-tuning

To disentangle the effects of structured large-scale pretraining from noise-driven initialization dynamics and to investigate whether random noise warm-up can be combined with conventional data pretraining in a fine-tuning setup, we conducted experiments using ResNet models under both training from scratch and fine-tuning. This design enabled a direct comparison between ImageNet-based pretraining, random noise warm-up and their combination. In all cases, models were compared with and without the random noise warm-up phase described above, whereas keeping downstream optimization procedures identical within each setting.

For the scratch setting, ResNet models were randomly initialized and trained on CIFAR-10. Standard data augmentation (random crop and horizontal flip) was applied during training. Optimization was performed using stochastic gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.1, momentum of 0.9 and cosine annealing for learning rate scheduling. The batch size was 256. In the warm-up condition, the network was first trained for five epochs on Gaussian random inputs. Subsequently, the model was trained on CIFAR-10 for 50 epochs. The control condition omitted the warm-up phase and was trained directly on CIFAR-10 for 50 epochs. All other experimental factors were held constant to isolate the effect of the warm-up phase.

For the fine-tuning setting, we used an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18, replacing the final fully connected layer with a newly initialized ten-class classifier. CIFAR-10 inputs were resized to the ImageNet input resolution (224 × 224). Standard data augmentation (random crop and horizontal flip) was applied during training. Optimization was performed using stochastic gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.01, momentum of 0.9, weight decay of 0.0001 and cosine annealing. The batch size was 256. In the warm-up condition, only the newly added classifier layer was updated during the random noise warm-up phase, whereas the pretrained backbone remained frozen. This phase was conducted for five epochs. Afterwards, all the layers were unfrozen, and the entire network was jointly fine-tuned on CIFAR-10 for 20 epochs. In the control condition, the model used the same pretrained weights but skipped the warm-up phase, and was fine-tuned for the same 20 epochs.
Visualization of confidence in toy model and high-dimensional networks

As a proof of concept, we used a simple toy model network with a 2D input space. The input space is defined as the span of two features, each varying between –10 and 10. This allows us to visualize the input space in the 2D plane, where the x axis represents the first feature, and the y axis represents the second feature. The neural network model consists of three layers, with a hidden layer of size 10. It outputs a 2D vector for binary classification. The model is initialized using He initialization.

We trained this toy model network with 2D random noise. To investigate the origin of the initial overconfidence in randomly initialized networks, we conducted experiments using fully connected models with rectified linear unit nonlinearities and batch normalization in hidden layers. Network weights were initialized using He initialization. We systematically varied either the number of output classes and fixing network depth, or the network depth and fixing the read-out size. For each configuration, we evaluated the network on the CIFAR-10 dataset and recorded logits, SoftMax probabilities, predicted labels and confidence before random noise warm-up. The same quantities were measured again after the warm-up phase. We further analysed changes in the distribution of logits and confidence values induced by random noise warm-up. To assess whether these phenomena generalize beyond fully connected architectures, we extended the analysis to ResNet families, DenseNet families and vision transformers.
OOD detection task

To design the OOD detection task, we used CIFAR-10 as the ID dataset for training. For the OOD samples, we selected SVHN, which also has a 32 × 32 × 3 input size. After training the model on CIFAR-10, we measured the confidence of the network for both ID and OOD samples. On the basis of the measured confidence, we distinguished between ID and OOD samples by setting an arbitrary threshold. If the confidence exceeded the threshold, the sample was predicted as an ID sample; if the confidence was lower than the threshold, it was predicted as an OOD sample. Since the performance of OOD detection depends on the chosen threshold, we varied the threshold continuously from 0 to 1 and measured the corresponding false-positive rate and true-positive rate. We then plotted the detection performance as an ROC curve, with the true-positive rate plotted against the false-positive rate. To quantitatively assess the detection performance, we measured the AUROC, which serves as a metric for the performance of OOD detection.
